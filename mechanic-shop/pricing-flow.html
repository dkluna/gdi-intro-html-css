Pricing Assistant Flow

How a car goes from the tech's inspection form to a correct, priced parts list in Mitchell 1, with an AI assistant doing the lookups and a person approving every order.

Today

The pricer decodes everything by hand

  • Reads the paper form, sometimes in Spanish shorthand
  • Works out which version of the part fits from Worldpac's small print
  • Guesses quantities: each, pair, set or box
  • Owner double-checks the work; cars wait days to be priced
With the assistant

The pricer reviews a list that's already right

  • Findings are read and translated for them
  • The right variant is picked from the VIN and fitment notes, or one clear question goes to the tech
  • Quantities come from how each part is sold
  • Owner only looks at what's flagged

One car, start to finish

Tech Pricer Assistant Owner / ops Customer

  1. Tech inspects and marks the form

    Tech

    Same paper form as today, in Spanish or English. ✕ needs it now ! soon ✓ OK

    Later: the tech talks to a tablet instead of writing.

  2. Pricer hands it to the assistant

    Pricer

    A phone photo of the form and the Mitchell 1 worksheet, on the office computer where the assistant runs with the shop's own Worldpac login.

  3. Reads the findings

    Assistant

    Translates the shop's Spanish ("balatas", "flecha", "piña"), separates what the customer came in for from the extra red and yellow items, and turns each one into a repair line with side and axle.

    Catches: a red or yellow item that never got priced, like a missed alignment.

  4. Identifies the exact car from the VIN

    Assistant

    Checks the VIN for copying errors, decodes year, trim, engine and drive, and trusts the VIN over what was written.

    Catches: "2024 Acura TSX" on a worksheet. The TSX ended in 2014, so the VIN decides what the car really is.

  5. Picks the right version in Worldpac

    Assistant

    Reads every option and every fitment note ("only with electronic parking brake", "330 mm rotor", "from 03/2019"). If one option fits, it says why. If two could fit, it asks the tech one question instead of guessing:

    “Mide el disco delantero: ¿12.6 o 13.0 pulgadas?” Measure the front rotor: 12.6 or 13.0 inches? ↺ Tech answers or sends a photo, and the assistant finishes the line.
    Catches: wrong pads or rotors for the trim level. Brakes are the shop's most common ordering mistake.

  6. Sets quantities and prices

    Assistant
    • Quantity from how the part is sold: pads = 1 axle set, rotors = 2 each, plugs = one per cylinder.
    • Price from the shop's margin matrix: 50% margin up to $250 cost, 31% above. A $100 part sells for $200.
    • Labor stays with Mitchell. The assistant notes rust or overlap, never invents hours.
    Catches: ordering 10 when 2 were needed.

  7. Pricer reviews, then exports as usual

    Pricer

    The pricer checks the list, selects those exact parts in Worldpac, and uses the existing Export to send them into Mitchell 1. Nothing new to learn in Mitchell.

    Person approves · assistant never orders or exports

  8. Owner checks only the flags, then the customer decides

    Owner / opsCustomer

    Flagged lines (uncertain fit, odd quantity, high cost, core charges) go to the owner or ops manager. Then the usual call to the customer, line by line.

  9. After approval: find a cheaper source

    AssistantPricer

    Checks the other vendors for the same quality, a similar warranty and on-time delivery. The customer's price never changes; only the shop's cost does. The pricer or owner places the order.

    Example: Worldpac $142 → another vendor $98, saves $44, arrives tomorrow. The owner finds a cheaper source about 30% of the time today.

Where people stay in control

No orders

The assistant reads and recommends. Ordering, carts and the export to Mitchell stay with the pricer.

No guessing

Unsure fit becomes one specific question to the tech, never the most likely part.

No made-up numbers

Costs come from Worldpac's screen, labor from Mitchell. Missing numbers are marked as missing.

Flags go up

Odd quantities, high-dollar lines and uncertain fits go to the owner or ops manager first.

Runs on the office computer with the shop's existing Worldpac and Mitchell 1 accounts. The tech tablet app comes later.
